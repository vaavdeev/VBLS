# Frequently Asked Questions about VBLS

This document collects the questions that typically come up on a first reading of the monograph *Viability Boundaries of Living Systems: A Phenomenological Thermodynamic Theory* — together with answers. Sections follow the structure of the monograph; each answer points to the relevant section for a fuller treatment.

---

## 1. The finite lifetime theorem (section 9, Appendix A)

**Isn't it obvious that any system with irreversible damage will eventually fail? What does the theorem actually add?**

Even within the class of systems VBLS considers, finiteness is not automatic: if the initial level of damage is zero, the export of damage always keeps pace with its production, and the resource inflow and the restoration of the reserve are sufficient, a system could in principle sustain a stationary state indefinitely. The theorem proves that, for any system that has accumulated damage even once, the specific structure of the equations rules out indefinite persistence for any bounded external resource flow. What carries the proof is the mechanism of irreversible memory: the record of damage does not decay, so the accumulated integral grows without bound and the efficiency of resource assimilation tends to zero. The separation of timescales between the fast integrity potential and the slow repair reserve does not enter the proof itself — it serves only as a motivating picture and in the treatment of the second death channel.

The value of the result lies less in the bare fact that lifetime is finite than in what the theorem delivers: an explicit bound in terms of the system's parameters, which certifies finiteness but does not set the scale of lifespan; a proof that the guarantee of finiteness comes from the irreversibility of the trace that damage leaves on assimilation efficiency, not from the mere accumulation of damage; and a foundation from which the viability boundary is later derived (section 11).

**If repair is perfect and damage no longer grows, why is the system still mortal?**

In the theory, damage means only what has been irreversibly lost. Whatever repair manages to remove in time does not enter this count and leaves no trace. So after the first irreversible damage, perfect repair can hold the level of damage constant but cannot bring it back to zero. From then on the memory mechanism acts. As long as damage is present, it slowly and irreversibly lowers the assimilation efficiency, that is, the fraction of the external inflow with which the system replenishes its integrity potential. The decline is set not by the growth of damage but by how long the damage is present, so it continues at a constant level too. The inflow is bounded, so its assimilated part tends to zero over time, while a living system's loss of potential never falls below a positive level. In finite time the potential falls to the threshold below which self-maintenance is impossible. If, however, repair keeps pace with everything from the start and there is no irreversible damage at all, the theorem does not promise death.

Two caveats. This conclusion rests on the assumption that the trace in assimilation efficiency is never erased; if it is erased over time, as in organisms with continuous renewal of their structure (see the question about hydra), finiteness is not guaranteed. And finiteness does not mean early death: the theorem's bound certifies finiteness but does not set the lifespan, and in the human calibration the second channel, depletion of the repair reserve, acts first.

**Doesn't the non-degeneracy condition amount to a loophole that makes the theorem unfalsifiable?**

No. The non-degeneracy condition (structural entropy becomes positive at least once) is an explicit restriction on the theorem's domain of applicability — the same kind of restriction as requiring "initial data away from equilibrium" in ordinary differential-equation problems. The degenerate case is a system in which damage has never become fixed: the initial structural entropy is zero, and the export of damage never falls below its production. Such a trajectory is admissible, but along it the system does not age either: its assimilation efficiency stays at the starting level. Whenever the non-degeneracy condition holds, structural entropy never returns to zero, the accumulated history of damage grows without bound, and the assimilation efficiency decays to zero regardless of what happens in the environment (within bounded flows).

**One of the two death channels (exhaustion of the repair reserve) holds only under an additional condition that does not follow from the theory's basic assumptions. Does that mean this channel isn't rigorously proved?**

Yes, and the theorem's own structure states this honestly. The two death mechanisms in VBLS carry different proof status. The channel through exhaustion of the integrity potential is proved unconditionally — this is the theorem's primary result. The channel through exhaustion of the repair reserve is subtler. For a system that has accumulated damage even once, the reserve threshold itself is always reached: if the solution of the equations is continued past the moment when the integrity potential is finally exhausted, restoration of the reserve is switched off there and the reserve declines to its threshold. What is guaranteed only under an additional condition (structural entropy exceeding a specific threshold) is something else — that the reserve runs out before the potential, that is, that death comes through this channel. That condition is expressed through the system's parameters and is physically motivated, but it is not derivable from the same basic assumptions as the first channel. Both channels matter for the complete picture (the second dominates in nature), but it would be inaccurate to say both are proved "equally rigorously."

**Are the theory's parameters simply fitted to the data rather than grounded in physics?**

This is the standard position for a phenomenological theory: parameters are calibrated against data rather than derived from molecular mechanics from scratch — classical thermodynamics works the same way. What matters is that VBLS's parameters are not pulled from thin air: two of them have a biological anchor. The rate of spontaneous repair-reserve degradation is fixed before calibration and agrees with the rate of telomere shortening (Whittemore et al., 2019); for the rate of structural damage production, data on the accumulation of somatic mutations (Cagan et al., 2022) set only the order of magnitude, and the value itself is fitted in the calibration. The rest are partly constrained by the theory itself (all of them are positive, for instance, and the restoration rate of the reserve is at most of the order of its spontaneous wear rate), and their precise values are set by calibration on human lifespan; the rate of memory degradation has no direct independent measurement (section 17.1). What matters more is that the calibration uses a single species while the comparisons are interspecific: at fixed parameters the theory is consistent with two independent interspecific regularities and with five more with caveats (section 17.1.3). These comparisons rest on assumptions about transferring parameters from species to species, and they test the theory together with those assumptions (section 17).

**The proved part of the theorem (through the integrity potential), once real numbers are substituted, yields a lifetime estimate that arrives far later than what is actually observed — in humans, more than two orders of magnitude later. Does this mean the theory is predicting a lifetime of thousands of years, or is the theorem's result simply empty — not predicting anything concrete?**

Neither. This estimate is not a lifetime prediction at all, but a "just-in-case" mathematical proof: it answers the question of whether death is still guaranteed if only the weaker of the two death mechanisms (exhaustion of the integrity potential) were operating, with the second, dominant mechanism in nature (exhaustion of the repair reserve) hypothetically switched off. The answer is yes, but not for a very long time — which is exactly why the resulting number is so large. This demonstrates that the theorem does not secretly rely only on realistic biology to guarantee finiteness: finiteness holds even in this artificially stripped-down, least favorable scenario for the theory.

The real, observed scale of lifetime is set by the other mechanism — whichever of the two death channels actually triggers first (for the vast majority of species, this is exhaustion of the repair reserve) — combined with parameter calibration against data. This is the standard structure for a phenomenological theory: an existence result (analogous to the Carnot limit — an unreachable but universal bound on the efficiency of heat engines) plus a separate, calibrated quantitative model for the specific case at hand. It would be wrong to say that the unconditional estimate "predicts lifetime" — better to say that it proves the inevitability of a finite end with a large margin, while the quantitative prediction for specific species comes from a different part of the theory.

## 2. Two death channels

**The two death channels (through the integrity potential and through the repair reserve) are dynamically coupled to each other. Why treat them separately at all rather than as one phenomenon?**

Coupling does not imply reducibility. The two channels differ in variable, threshold, and observed outcome:

- **The repair-reserve channel** is typical of most mammals: the system dies when the repair reserve falls to its threshold, while the integrity potential is still high.
- **The integrity-potential channel** is consistent with what is seen in two long-lived species with a suppressed first channel: the system dies with a large share of its telomere reserve — an observed analogue of the repair reserve — still left. The caveat matters: there are only two such species, the assignment was made after consulting the data, and a statistical test of the two-channel structure requires an independent sample of at least thirty species. No such test has yet been performed.

These are distinct terminal events, and merging them into a single variable would erase that distinction. Moreover, the coupling between the channels is asymmetric: decay of the repair reserve does not depend directly on the integrity potential, while its restoration does; the reverse influence exists but is indirect. Finally, in the calibrated human model the two variables operate on different characteristic timescales: the integrity potential adjusts to current conditions quickly, while the repair reserve changes tens of times more slowly. In general this separation of timescales is not guaranteed and has to be checked for each calibration, but where it holds it makes a separate analysis natural.

The correct term for this relationship is not "independent" (too strong, given the real coupling between channels) but "functionally irreducible": neither channel can be derived from the other as a special case, and each carries its own, distinguishable contribution to explaining observed mortality.

**Why can the repair reserve be restored while assimilation efficiency cannot? Doesn't that look like an arbitrary assumption?**

It is indeed a strong assumption, but a physically grounded one. The repair reserve and assimilation efficiency are quantities of a different nature. The reserve is the capacity of the repair apparatus: it wears out and can be replenished. Assimilation efficiency is an integral characteristic of the state of the organism's entire coupling structure, and its decline reflects accumulated irreversible changes in molecular architecture across many levels at once. Restoring such an efficiency would require a mechanism structurally independent of the repair apparatus itself — and the basic model provides for no such separate mechanism. The assumption is strong, but its possible relaxation is explicitly flagged as a direction for extending the theory.

**The death channel through the integrity potential shows almost no sensitivity of the final lifetime to the parameters that govern it. Does this mean this channel can't be tested experimentally?**

Zero sensitivity is a property of one specific, and most common, regime (where the repair-reserve channel dominates) — not a sign that the mechanism itself is untestable. First, this is a regime property: as soon as dominance shifts to the integrity-potential channel (which can be modeled by weakening the mechanisms responsible for the first channel), the parameters of the second channel immediately become significant for the final lifetime. Second, there is an observational indication: in some long-lived species with a suppressed first channel, death occurs while the repair reserve is still high, which is consistent with the second channel at work. Third, the theory makes a quantitative, testable prediction about the conditions under which sensitivity to the second channel's parameters should become detectable.

**Isn't the spontaneous-decay rate parameter of the repair reserve just the well-known Hayflick limit (the cap on the number of cell divisions) under another name?**

No, these are quantities of different types and should not be conflated. The Hayflick limit is a threshold number of divisions — a dimensionless quantity. The spontaneous-decay-rate parameter in VBLS is exactly that: a rate, with dimension "inverse time"; in dividing cells it can be matched to the normalized rate of telomere shortening. The link between them is then mediated by how frequently a given tissue's cells divide. In the work the Hayflick limit is matched to the reserve threshold and the wear rate to the pace of approach to that threshold; these are fundamentally different roles within the model, and the matching itself is an interpretation.

**What exactly counts as the moment of death — exhaustion of the integrity potential, or of the repair reserve?**

Neither takes priority by itself: death is whichever of the two thresholds is reached first for the system in question. For the vast majority of species, the repair-reserve threshold is reached first, while the integrity potential is still high. In species with a suppressed first channel, it is the reverse — the integrity potential is exhausted first while the repair reserve is barely touched. In a state of dormancy, where the memory mechanism is effectively switched off and metabolism and dissipation are suppressed, finiteness of existence is still guaranteed — through the slow spontaneous decay of the repair reserve, which is not restored during dormancy.

## 3. The viability boundary and the three barriers

**The theory claims a sharp boundary between viable and non-viable configurations, while comparative biology shows a continuous spectrum of transitional forms (viruses, organelles, reduced symbionts). Isn't there a contradiction here?**

There is no contradiction, for two reasons. First, none of the objects listed sits on the boundary itself. Viruses fall outside the theory's domain altogether: they have neither repair of their own nor a resource flux of their own, and the relevant system is the infected host cell. Organelles and reduced symbionts, which do have a metabolism of their own, are described by the theory but lie below the boundary: as standalone systems they are not viable and exist at the host's expense. Second, the boundary is sharp only with respect to one specific system with a fixed architecture; the exact point where the boundary sits depends continuously on the system's parameters — and the continuity observed in comparative biology is precisely the continuity of evolutionary drift in that parameter space, not a blurring of the threshold for a given system.

**Why is a third viability barrier needed at all, if life apparently can start once the first two are satisfied?**

All three barriers are conditions on the moment of birth: they answer whether the system starts in a state able to sustain itself. Two conditions of different status are associated with the third barrier. The first is a resource condition, and it is this condition that forms the barrier: if, before the start, the available resource flow falls below a certain threshold, the memory mechanism is not fully provisioned, and the system begins life with a weakened assimilation efficiency. In the extreme case, when that efficiency is close to zero from the very start, the integrity potential begins to decline monotonically right from the outset, regardless of the state of the repair reserve, and the system, as a rule, dies through the same mechanism described in Section 1, without waiting for damage to accumulate. This happens even when the first two barriers are satisfied: they protect the system from other threats, but not from a resource shortfall that starves assimilation efficiency itself.

The second condition is structural, and it is not a barrier: it determines whether the accumulation of damage can be described as a continuous process. If the system has few structural elements, damage accumulates in rare jumps and the moment of death becomes random. Both death channels nevertheless remain open: the very first irreversible damage leaves a trace in assimilation efficiency that is never erased, and the memory mechanism keeps working.

It's worth not confusing the number of barriers with the number of death channels: there are three barriers because they protect three different variables of the system, but only two death channels (Section 2) — the second and third barriers both, at different stages, guard the same channel: exhaustion of the integrity potential.

**Why doesn't the characteristic memory timescale depend on the characteristic energy-turnover timescale? Intuitively, one would expect metabolic turnover rate to affect how fast the memory mechanism degrades.**

These are two parameters describing different processes in different variables of the system: the energy-turnover time characterizes the integrity potential, while the memory timescale characterizes assimilation efficiency. There is a dynamical link between the variables through accumulated structural entropy, but this does not make one time a direct function of the other when deriving the estimate: the condition for a significant drop in assimilation efficiency is derived directly, without any assumption about how these two system timescales relate to each other.

**There is a formula where the rate of terminal decline is expressed through the characteristic level of structural entropy "on the late plateau" — and that level itself seems to be set by the very dynamics that includes the moment of death. Isn't this circular?**

There is no circularity, but there is no independence of the kind the question presupposes either. The characteristic plateau level is the level of damage in the late part of the trajectory. The balance of damage production and export sets not that level itself but the moment when damage stops growing; the level is whatever has accumulated by then, and it depends on the trajectory. The formula for terminal time is therefore an order-of-magnitude estimate at a given level, consistent with the trajectory, not a prediction of lifespan from the parameters alone: the lifetime itself is obtained by integrating the equations.

**There is a lemma stating that the integrity potential cannot increase under a certain condition — but it is formulated for the basic, simplest version of the system. Real organisms have more elaborate active self-maintenance mechanisms. Does the lemma lose force for them?**

For systems of the class for which the finiteness theorem is proved, no. The description with active restoration of the memory mechanism concerns how its initial level is formed before life begins; over life itself the trace of damage in assimilation efficiency is never erased, and the proof of the lemma applies as it stands. Organisms in which restoration continues after the start do not belong to this class; for them the lemma's conclusion survives under a stronger condition: if the incoming resource flow is consistently below the dissipation at the threshold even at full assimilation efficiency, the integrity potential still declines monotonically down to the threshold. The lemma itself is formulated for a constant inflow and concerns the initial growth phase, not viability: with a gradually rising inflow the potential may at first decline; if this deficit is smaller than the starting margin of the potential, the margin covers it, and the potential stays above the threshold. The lemma's practical value lies in analyzing borderline, limiting configurations, not typical living systems.

**The viability boundary is defined within a class of systems that already presupposes a particular autocatalytic, dissipative architecture with memory. Isn't this then a viability boundary within an already "life-like" class, rather than a genuine demarcation between the living and the non-living?**

The objection rests on a silent assumption that life exists outside this architecture. The work claims the opposite: no known living system contradicts its conditions, and no life is known outside this architecture. The demarcation between living and non-living is therefore two-layered: first, the architectural conditions themselves, which set the domain of the theory, and second, the conditions of a living start within that architecture, the first of which is the position relative to the viability boundary. The architecture alone is not enough for life: a living system repairs itself — it restores its repair capacity from its own metabolism, and near the critical threshold faster than the capacity wears out. The point is that it restores not only damaged structure but the repair apparatus itself: devices can remove damage too — a material with capsules of healing agent, for instance, or memory with error correction — but they do not restore their repair apparatus. In this sense existing mechanistic systems do not repair themselves: they are repaired from outside or use up a non-renewable stock; their own restoration is absent or negligible, so as standalone systems they lie outside the class the theory describes or below the viability boundary, and the work does not consider them separately. (The class of systems for which the finiteness theorem is proved is the part of this architecture in which the trace of damage is never erased; organisms with continuous structural renewal satisfy the same conditions, but their memory of damage fades.) What fails these conditions is either dissipative structures without memory (which can persist indefinitely, carry no history — order without memory, not life in the sense of the theory) or non-autonomous subsystems such as viruses, which have neither repair of their own nor a resource flux of their own. (Organelles and obligate symbionts with a metabolism of their own are not such subsystems: the theory describes them, but as standalone systems they lie below the viability boundary and exist at the host's expense.) No known living object violates these conditions — so the demarcation covers life as we actually know it, which is an ordinary and honest status for a phenomenological theory of this kind.

**Some animals — hydra, planarians — barely age at all: their mortality does not rise with age, and they look biologically immortal. Doesn't this refute the theorem that lifetime is always finite?**

No — and the theory treats this case explicitly. The mechanism that makes life finite relies on the accumulated history of damage **growing** over the system's lifetime. In organisms with continuous cell renewal, replacement does two things: it removes damaged elements, keeping the level of damage bounded, and it renews the carriers of the trace themselves, so that old damage gradually stops affecting assimilation efficiency. The accumulated history therefore does not grow without bound but **saturates** at a finite, low level. As long as it stays below the critical threshold, the memory-related death channel simply never switches on — and mortality remains **constant** rather than rising. This is consistent with what is seen in hydra: age-specific mortality does not increase and there is no Gompertz component (Schaible et al., 2015). Such an organism is therefore not an unexplained paradox for the theory but something it **describes** — though not by the base system for which the theorem is proved, but by its generalization with a fading memory. Such a memory may be ascribed to an organism only when its renewal is established independently, not inferred from the very absence of aging; and it switches the memory channel off only while the level of damage stays bounded.

A deeper question remains — about identity, the analogue of the Ship of Theseus paradox: if all of a hydra's cells are eventually replaced, is it the same organism? In the theory the identity of a system is tied to its organization, not to its material: a system remains the same as long as a continuous causal and organizational link connects its successive states, and the replacement of cells does not by itself break that identity (Section 2.6). A hydra is therefore one system, but one with a fading memory of damage; the finiteness theorem, proved for systems whose memory does not fade, does not cover it and so does not contradict it. And at the level where history does persist — the individual cell — lifetime is finite: in *E. coli* the cell inheriting the old pole ages and dies, and when the rate of damage accumulation exceeds a threshold, the aging lineages become mortal while the rejuvenating lineages of the same population remain immortal (Proenca et al., 2019). Finally, the nonzero constant mortality of non-senescent organisms is itself due to random events outside the deterministic core of the theory: real hydra still die — just not of aging.

**Tissue regeneration, transplantation, replacement of cells from a pool of stem cells — doesn't this refute the theory?**

No. Replacement does two different things, and the theory distinguishes them. If replacement removes damage from the structure, it is repair: the level of irreversible damage stops growing but does not fall. If, instead, the carriers of the trace themselves are replaced — the part of the structure on which assimilation efficiency depends — past damage loses its connection with the current state. That is no longer repair but forgetting: the memory of damage fades, and such a system is described by the base model's generalization, for which finiteness is not guaranteed (see the question about hydra). What would refute the theory is something else: a stable recovery of the irreversible component of assimilation efficiency in an adult organism of advanced age after current damage has been removed, without replacement of the carriers and without a change in the accumulated history (criterion F1, Section 17.0). Systems with continuous renewal of structure do not count as a refutation if the renewal is established independently. Transplantation of a tissue or organ is an intervention from outside, and the system's own dynamics does not describe it.

## 4. Structural entropy and cumulative (non-decaying) memory

**The system repairs itself. Why, then, does structural entropy $\Sigma$ never decrease?**

Because $\Sigma$ counts only what has been irreversibly lost. Repair clears damage while it is still reversible: it restores structures, replaces molecules, recycles components. Such damage never enters $\Sigma$. What remains in $\Sigma$ is only what repair failed to clear in time, and that, by definition, can no longer be repaired. Repair can therefore slow or stop the growth of $\Sigma$, but it cannot lower it. This is a property of how the variable is defined, not a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics: the second law constrains the sign of damage production, not the difference between production and removal (Section 9.6). Nor does repair itself always return the structure to its original state. For example, in one proposed pathway of RNA repair a ribozyme ligates the damaged molecule to itself through a non-canonical 2′–5′ linkage: the information is preserved, but the molecule is no longer the same (Section 3.2). This is an example, not a foundation of the theory.

**Is structural entropy $\Sigma$ the same thing as Boltzmann entropy? How would one measure it in practice?**

No, the two should not be equated. $\Sigma$ is a latent variable: it is not tied to a single measurable biomarker, but defined through general properties (monotonicity, normalization, and so on) that any concrete realization of it must satisfy. Classical Boltzmann entropy has a single operational measurement protocol; $\Sigma$ has no such single protocol — instead, there are admissible proxy measures, such as the accumulation of somatic mutations. Not every marker of aging qualifies, and an individual marker usually reflects several variables of the theory at once — a measure of $\Sigma$ is therefore assembled from several markers, selected for cumulativeness and for a known contribution of each. Which proxy to use in a given application is a separate, applied question outside the basic theory.

**Has the dependence of assimilation efficiency on accumulated history of structural entropy, rather than on its current value, been proved by independent biological data?**

There is data structurally consistent with this hypothesis (epigenetic clocks in particular), but it does not prove the uniqueness of this specific, integral form of dependence — the same data is in principle explainable by other mechanisms. For a phenomenological theory, it is enough that the value of the assimilation efficiency is set by the accumulated history rather than by the instantaneous value of the variable. The equation itself is local: it contains only the current $\Sigma$, and the history is accumulated by its solution. A finer, quantitative test of the specific form of this dependence remains an open question awaiting further data.

**Does $\Sigma\to1$ mark the moment of death?**

No. $\Sigma=1$ is the upper end of the scale, not an event of death; the equations do not cap it, and in principle a trajectory can cross it. In practice, death occurs earlier: for typical parameters, $\Sigma$ at the moment of death is noticeably below one. This is a property of the trajectories of the theory; it will become a testable prediction once the limiting value of the observable damage markers is fixed independently of the moment of death. Death in VBLS is defined not by $\Sigma$ approaching one, but by reaching one of two real thresholds — exhaustion of the integrity potential or of the repair reserve — within finite time.

**Is structural entropy bounded above by 1 through the dynamics itself, or is this just a convention of scale?**

It is a convention of scale, not a dynamically guaranteed property: the equation governing the growth of $\Sigma$ contains no mechanism that would automatically keep it at or below one. This does not weaken the finite lifetime theorem: the proof relies only on the monotonicity of structural entropy and on damage having left a trace at least once (structural entropy having become positive), and never uses an upper bound of one at all — that upper bound serves only as a substantive interpretation of the scale (the value 1 corresponds to complete loss of structural identity; as a rule, death by one of the two channels occurs earlier).

**Why do we need a separate memory variable (assimilation efficiency) at all, rather than just using the system's current state — wouldn't that be simpler?**

There is a hidden ambiguity in the word "state" here. If "current state" means the full set of all four system variables, including assimilation efficiency, then the objection does not actually conflict with the theory: the system is indeed Markovian with respect to this complete set. The real question is different: can one make do with a truncated set of variables, without a separate memory variable — that is, can assimilation efficiency be expressed as a function of only structural entropy, the integrity potential, and the repair reserve? It turns out the answer is no: assimilation efficiency depends on the time-accumulated integral of structural entropy, not on its current value, and two different trajectories can arrive at the same point in the remaining three variables while carrying different accumulated integrals — meaning different resulting assimilation efficiencies. So this variable does carry genuinely independent information.

The precise mathematical form of this dependence on history is itself an open question subject to refinement (on the data currently available, it cannot always be statistically distinguished from certain alternative descriptions). The need for a memory variable as such is a question of another level: if the rate of aging depends on prior history and not only on the current level of damage, this cannot be described without a separate variable, whichever formula eventually proves correct. Empirically, such a dependence on history is consistent with data on adverse childhood experiences, but there is no decisive test yet: it would require comparing systems with the same current level of damage and different histories.

**Is this model of memory supported by independent biological data?**

There are consistent observations: the decline of mitochondrial efficiency and of maximal oxygen consumption with age, and the roughly linear accumulation of somatic mutations in mid-life, all match the direction of the theory's predictions. But this is support for the general direction, not proof of the uniqueness of the specific mathematical form of the dependence — a stronger test of that kind would require data that is not yet available in sufficient quantity.

## 5. Parameters and empirical testing

**Aren't seven calibrated parameters simply overfitting to the data?**

The key difference from overfitting is that this is not repeated testing on the same sample but qualitatively different comparisons with one and the same fixed set of parameters: two independent regularities in two datasets on different species, and five more with caveats (section 17.1.3). Their status is not uniform, and the work distinguishes it: some are consistency checks, some are retrospective comparisons, and the theory's own distinctive consequences are stated as falsification criteria that await data. Nor were the parameters tuned comparison by comparison: two of them have a biological anchor (the rate of spontaneous reserve wear is fixed before calibration and agrees with the rate of telomere shortening, while for the rate of damage production the rate of mutation accumulation sets only the order of magnitude), and the rest are calibrated once — on human lifespan — and then held fixed. The comparisons themselves are interspecific and rest on assumptions about transferring parameters from species to species, so they test the theory together with those assumptions.

**Numerical simulation shows that only about a third of randomly sampled parameter sets (within a reasonable range) fall within the real demographic corridor of human life expectancy. Doesn't this indicate an unstable calibration?**

No — this is the expected fraction, not a sign of instability, and here is why. The demographic corridor used in this comparison is fairly narrow — comparable in width to just one standard deviation of the resulting model distribution. For a distribution centered exactly on the target, a window of this width by construction captures only the central portion of the values — ordinary normal-distribution arithmetic predicts roughly a third to two-fifths, which is exactly what is observed. The remaining cases are not "misses" but natural spread into a range that is itself biologically plausible. At the same time, the overall spread in predicted lifetime, under a substantial (about 20%) simultaneous variation of all parameters, turns out to be markedly narrower than the input spread — meaning the model actually compresses uncertainty rather than amplifying it.

**One statistical test shows that the proposed form of memory's dependence on history is statistically indistinguishable, on the data available, from simpler alternative curves. Does this mean the central idea of the work — cumulative memory — hasn't actually been tested at all?**

First, about the premise: that test does not compare forms of memory. It compares ways of describing the age-related decline of aerobic capacity, and the memory mechanism is not tested by it (Appendix D.2.9). Beyond that, it helps to separate three different levels of claim. The first is the memory mechanism itself as part of a rigorous mathematical result (Section 1): this is a proved fact about the structure of the equations, independent of what data happens to be available today. The second is the general empirical profile — that the rate of aging depends on history rather than only on the current level of damage: observations such as data on adverse childhood experiences are consistent with it, but there has been no decisive test yet, one with the same current level of damage and different histories. The third is the specific mathematical form of that dependence: on the data currently available it genuinely cannot be distinguished from a few reasonable alternatives, and this is openly acknowledged as a subject for further testing. Conflating these three levels is what creates the impression that "the central idea hasn't been tested": in fact the first level is proved, the second is consistent with the data but still awaits a decisive test, and the third is open.

**The cumulative, non-decaying memory mechanism is claimed as the theory's central novelty. But couldn't all the confirmed cross-species regularities be explained without it — by combining already-known laws (Kleiber's metabolic scaling, the correlation between telomere length and lifespan, classical reliability theory)? Doesn't Occam's razor cut this mechanism away as redundant?**

The honest answer: the regularities confirmed to date do not, on their own, single out memory. The product of mutation-accumulation rate and lifespan, the link between telomere-shortening rate and lifespan, can, if one wishes, be reproduced without a separate memory mechanism. As for the quantity with the dimension of action introduced in the work, its scaling with mass is not an independent check at all: under the adopted method of computation it reduces algebraically to the metabolic allometry, and the work now states this outright. Were the theory resting on results of that kind alone, Occam's razor would be fair.

But the memory mechanism has its own, qualitatively distinguishable signature — a non-monotonic dependence of lifespan on the sensitivity to accumulated history (the parameter $\alpha$). A memoryless model predicts a monotonic gain: the more slowly damage accumulates, the longer life extends, without bound. The memory feedback predicts something different — an optimum, at which both too-high and too-low sensitivity shorten life alike. None of the three competing laws contains a memory parameter, and none can produce such an optimum in principle: this is precisely where memory is testable and irreplaceable. The existence of this optimum is, as of today, a numerically supported hypothesis rather than a proved theorem, and the work flags this explicitly.

**Does the theory say anything about why caloric restriction extends lifespan — a long-known experimental effect? And if so, why haven't organisms evolved toward the greatest possible longevity?**

Yes, and the two questions are connected. The life-extending effect of moderate dietary restriction emerges in the theory as a direct consequence of its structure, with no separate "aging program": a reduced metabolic flux slows the growth of structural entropy and, with it, the degradation of the memory mechanism, so lifespan increases. Crucially, this is not an unbounded gain: the effect has an optimum, and excessive restriction is as harmful as excessive consumption — a prediction that distinguishes memory from simple "less damage is always better" models.

This also answers the second question. Selection maximizes not longevity but fitness — reproductive success. Its optimum in memory sensitivity lies below the optimum for longevity: the extra lifespan gained by shifting toward maximal longevity falls mostly in the post-reproductive period, while the very mechanism that yields it also lowers the energetics of reproduction. The theory therefore predicts that the evolutionarily settled memory sensitivity of a species should lie below the value that would maximize its lifespan — organisms turn out to be systematically "under-long-lived" relative to their own theoretical ceiling.

**The theory has been tested only on small, uniquely available samples of data (a handful of species, dozens of individuals), without large-scale independent validation. Is that really enough for a fundamental theory?**

Sample size here is objectively limited — these are, at present, the only datasets of their kind that exist anywhere in the world (for example, with a direct, cross-species-comparable measurement of telomere shortening rate), not a result of cherry-picking convenient data. It also helps to keep the genre in mind: a fundamental phenomenological theory (as classical thermodynamics once was) does not need large-scale empirical validation to be mathematically sound — its value lies in rigor, qualitative laws, and falsifiability. Even so, this work's empirical testing spans several datasets, which is already more than is strictly required for a theory of this kind, and not all of its results are positive. In the largest of the tests — hundreds of mammal and bird species in two data compilations, AnAge and Escala — the prediction that lifespan is inversely proportional to specific metabolism is rejected by the data in its full form: lifespan depends on metabolic power and body mass not only through their ratio (Section D.5.9). Broader cross-species validation is flagged as a task awaiting the corresponding future data.

**On the plots, the rate of damage accumulation looks flat, almost zero, at the start of life. Is this a visualization error?**

No, this is a real and meaningful feature of the model. Early in life, the repair reserve is still high and barely needed, so the rate of damage export fully offsets its production, and the net accumulation of structural entropy is close to zero. Mutation data for humans and horses agree with this: a regression fitted on mid-life data and extended backward crosses zero not at birth but several years later; in three other species with small samples the model and the data disagree on this point (section 17.3).

**Toward the end of life, the rate of damage accumulation declines on the plot. Doesn't this contradict the idea of "acceleration" of aging near the end of life — and doesn't it, if anything, match the observed deceleration of aging in very old age?**

It is important to distinguish the rate of accumulation from the accumulated quantity itself: structural entropy keeps growing monotonically right up to death, while its *rate* of growth in the calibrated human model peaks around age 32, stays nearly constant between 20 and 50, and declines after 60 — by about a factor of three by the moment of death. This comes from a competition of two effects: damage production follows the integrity potential and falls with it, while damage export falls with the reserve; after the potential peaks, and especially after the decline of resource inflow that the calibration assumes after age 60, the first effect prevails. The depth of the decline is set by the system together with that assumed decline of inflow: with constant inflow the rate at death is only about 40% below its maximum. This is qualitatively consistent with the observation that we age more slowly as we get older, but it has no direct bearing on the deceleration of mortality growth in very old age: such a deceleration does not follow from the theory's equations by themselves and requires variation between individuals. The system still dies — through the repair-reserve channel, not because of the rate of damage accumulation itself.

**Why does the work include a separate test on a minimal synthetic genome (JCVI-syn3.0), given that the main empirical testing has already been done on continuous aging trajectories across several species?**

This is a comparison of a different kind, and its standing is more modest than that of the main tests. The main verification concerns continuous quantities — the shape of curves, lifetime itself. Here what is compared is an estimate of the size of a minimal genome: the metabolic core is taken from comparative genomics, and the number of repair genes is obtained by enumerating the types of DNA damage that do not substitute for one another; the theory's condition on the export of damage (Barrier II) serves this estimate as a consistency check, not as the source of the number. The result is 293 genes against the 324 genes of known function in a synthetic cell obtained by a twenty-year experimental search for a minimal genome. The work classifies this as retrospective consistency rather than verification: the calculation was carried out after the experimental result was known, and it does not test the position of the viability boundary — that requires measured rates of wear and restoration of the reserve.

**On the plot of the integrity potential, it briefly dips at the very start of life before rising. Is this a modeling error?**

No, this is an expected transient. At the very first moment, the incoming resource flow in the model has not yet reached full strength (consumption has not yet "matured"), while baseline dissipation is already underway — so the integrity potential briefly declines before the inflow begins to exceed dissipation and it turns to growth. This effect is the same in all species considered: under consistent scaling the relative depth of the dip is identical for all of them — about 9% of the starting value. In substance, it corresponds to a temporarily negative energy balance at the very start of life, before the capacity to assimilate resources has fully matured, and has nothing to do with a quite different, far more specific biological phenomenon such as embryonic rejuvenation of epigenetic age.

## 6. Phenomenological foundations

**Where does the specific quadratic form of the dissipation function come from? Is it a rigorous derivation from Onsager thermodynamics?**

Not a rigorous derivation, but a structural reference point. The dissipation function has two terms, one linear and one quadratic in the potential $E$. Onsager's linear irreversible thermodynamics (1931) corresponds to the linear term: near equilibrium, dissipation is proportional to the stock of potential itself, and for small $E$ this term is exact. The quadratic term is the most economical correction for the nonlinear growth of dissipation far from equilibrium; it cannot be derived from Onsager's theory, which holds only close to equilibrium. Importantly, the finite lifetime theorem itself is proved for any dissipation function with the same general qualitative properties (zero dissipation at zero potential, strictly monotonic growth): the specific form matters for the quantitative calculations, not for the proof.

**What exactly is the "integrity potential" — is it energy? What are its units?**

It is a phenomenological quantity — a potential of self-maintenance — not "energy" in the narrow classical sense, and not the Gibbs free energy of a single molecule. It shares its units with energy, but the theory deliberately does not specify which particular molecular substrate underlies it in any given case — a normal feature of a phenomenological quantity, not a shortcoming of the theory. Depending on the type of system, this concept naturally reduces to different more concrete quantities: for instance, in biochemical systems, to the overall Gibbs free energy store of the system.

## 7. Mathematical formalization

**The equation for the efficiency of resource assimilation appears, from the text, to depend on the integral of structural entropy over the system's entire past history. But then it is an integro-differential equation, and the system itself is non-Markovian: it cannot be closed with a finite number of variables without adding one more, a "counter" of accumulated history. Doesn't this contradict the theory's claim that four variables suffice and that the system is Markovian?**

There is no contradiction; it arises from conflating the equation with its solution. The equation governing the dynamics of resource-assimilation efficiency is local: its right-hand side depends only on the current values of the structural entropy and of the efficiency itself — not on any integral. It is an ordinary differential equation, not an integro-differential one. The integral over history appears only when the equation is solved: the solution of a linear first-order equation is always the exponential of the integral of its coefficient — a standard property of such equations, not a sign of memory within the equation itself. That the solution "remembers" the history does not make the equation non-Markovian.

In the full set of four variables the system is Markovian: knowing all four quantities at any instant determines its entire future uniquely. The history is not needed separately — it is already recorded in the current value of the resource-assimilation efficiency. Two organisms with the same structural entropy but a different history of its accumulation have different assimilation efficiencies, and therefore occupy different points of the four-dimensional state space, not the same one. Here the memory is not hidden outside the state — it is visible directly as the value of the fourth variable.

The fifth variable — a "counter" of accumulated history — is indeed redundant. It is expressed uniquely (through a logarithm) in terms of the assimilation efficiency already present, and so carries no new information. Adding it would not rescue Markovianity (which already holds) but would only break the minimality of the description by introducing a superfluous degree of freedom. This is precisely why the statement "four variables are necessary and sufficient" is not weakened by this objection but strengthened: the fourth variable is necessary because it carries the memory, and the fifth is redundant because it would duplicate it.

Herein lies the substantive strength of the theory. Its memory is not an abstract mathematical integral hovering outside the system's physical state, but a materially embodied quantity: the actual efficiency with which an organism assimilates resource through its physical structures (membranes, enzyme complexes, mitochondria). Measurable physiological indicators correspond to it — for instance, maximal oxygen uptake or mitochondrial efficiency. The theory introduces no artificial counter of the past; it shows that the past is already physically inscribed in the organism's current state.

**But wouldn't it be more honest, and deeper, to call the system non-Markovian outright — since life really does carry a memory of its past?**

The physical intuition here is correct: life really does carry an irreversible memory, and the theory does not conceal this. But mathematically, calling the system non-Markovian would be inaccurate and, paradoxically, weaker. The memory in this theory does not decay: every past moment is weighted equally, and the accumulated integral only grows. It is exactly this non-decaying memory that permits a description by a finite number of variables — it collapses precisely into a local differential equation. An infinite-dimensional apparatus (function spaces) would be required for a memory with a general kernel, in which the weight of the past declines by an arbitrary law; even an exponentially forgetting memory still collapses into one additional equation. In the theory's base system the memory does not decay. Non-decaying memory and Markovianity in a four-dimensional space are not opposites but two sides of one coin.

A practical consequence: the proof of the finite-lifetime theorem proceeds by standard methods of the analysis of ordinary differential equations (differential inequalities, comparison theorems), without the heavy apparatus of functional analysis. Abandoning Markovianity would not make the theory deeper — it would contradict the theory's own local equation and needlessly complicate the proof. Non-Markovianity appears here only in a derived sense: if one eliminates the memory variable and describes only the remaining three quantities, the dependence on history becomes explicit — but this is a consequence of the couplings within the system, not a property of its basic equations.
